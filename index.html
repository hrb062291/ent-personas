01 · The idea

What a persona is (and isn't)

A persona is a decision-making tool. Every time your team argues about a feature, price, or message, you ask: "What would Maya do?"

✅ It is

  • Based on real conversations
  • Focused on goals, pains, and behaviors
  • Specific enough to say "no" to things
  • A living document you update

❌ It isn't

  • A demographic range ("ages 18–35")
  • A stereotype or a guess you never test
  • A list of fun but irrelevant trivia
  • Your ideal customer who agrees with you

🧭 Segment vs. persona

A customer segment is a group ("busy college students"). A persona is a single, representative person from that group, so the group becomes concrete. One segment can have 1–3 personas.

Proto-persona vs. validated persona. Your first draft is a proto-persona: your best guess. It becomes a validated persona only after customer interviews confirm (or correct) it. Expect your first draft to be wrong in interesting ways.

05 · Getting real data

Personas come from conversations

Draft a proto-persona.

Write down your assumptions in 15 minutes. Mark each one as a guess.

Find 5–10 real people

who match it. Classmates, online communities, people in line at the coffee shop.

Ask about the past, not the future.

What did they actually do last time? What did it cost them? What did they try?

Look for patterns.

If 6 of 8 people mention the same frustration, that's a persona pain. If 1 does, it's a note.

Rewrite the persona.

Replace guesses with evidence. Note how many interviews back each claim.

    08 · Watch out

    Six common persona mistakes

    The "everyone" persona

    "Anyone who wants to save time" describes every human alive. If your persona couldn't help you reject an idea, it's too broad. Narrow to the people who feel the pain most sharply, your early adopters.

    Demographics without behavior

    Age, gender, and income are easy to write but rarely explain why someone buys. Two 20-year-olds can behave completely differently. Lead with goals, pains, and what they do today.

    Writing yourself

    Founders often describe themselves. You might be a customer, but you're a sample of one, and you're biased. Interview people who aren't your friends.

    Never testing it

    A persona built entirely in a classroom is a hypothesis. Until you've talked to real people, label it a proto-persona and treat every line as a guess.

    Too many personas

    Five personas means no focus. Start with one primary persona for your earliest customers. Add others only when evidence shows a truly different group.

    Making it once and forgetting it

    Every round of interviews should update your persona. If it hasn't changed in a month and you've been talking to customers, you aren't listening closely.
